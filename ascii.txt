notes - common traps
The "Weightlessness" Trap (Animation Speed vs. Scale)

 If a character scales 5x larger, but their breathing, head turns, and posture shifts play at the exact same speed (1.0x animation playback), they immediately look like a weightless plastic toy rather than a colossal entity.
• animation playback speed and easing (AnimSpeed ≈ 1.0 / Sqrt(Scale)). Larger scale = slower, heavier, more deliberate movement with longer ease-out settling.
Camera Near/Far Clipping & Z-Fighting at Extremes
• At micro scale (or tight close-ups to measure tiny 2% growth gains), a standard camera Near Clip (0.1m) slices right through the character's nose or chest. Conversely, at macro scale (when shrinking the background or pulling the camera way back), distant walls flicker (Z-fighting) or vanish past the Far Clip plane.
• The Fix: Dynamically interpolate Camera3D.Near and Camera3D.alongside character scale (e.g., Near = 0.01f * currentScale), and avoid scaling non-uniformly (X != Y != Z) on parent nodes, which breaks physics colliders and normal maps.

Dynamic Focal Length (FOV) vs. Physical Dolly (The "Dolly Zoom" Effect)
•  Simply moving the camera backward (+Z) as the character grows keeps the character the same apparent size on screen, which accidentally cancels out the emotional feeling of growth.
• For subtle micro-gains: Keep the camera locked to an environmental doorframe or prop reference for 1–2 seconds so the player visually sees the character's head/shoulders rise past a fixed marker before the camera smoothly compensates.
• For colossal macro-surges: Combine a low-angle camera pitch (+Pitch looking up) with a wider FOV (50° -> 75°) as the camera pulls back—this exaggerates vertical perspective lines so the upper body towers over the lens.

Pivot Point Drift (Scaling from Pelvis vs. Scaling from Floor)
• Earlier we noted rotating around the Pelvis works great for airborne/chair spins. However, if you scale a standing or seated character from their Pelvis instead of their Floor Contact Point (Y = 0), their feet will push straight down through the floor (-Y) as they grow.
• Maintain a two-tier pivot:
	• Scale Anchor: Always at the surface contact point (the soles of the feet on the floor, or the bottom of the chair cushion at Y = 0).
	• Rotation/Orbit Pivot: A child node at the Pelvis / Center of Mass for camera tracking and mid-air spins.

Environmental Scale Anchors ("Banana for Scale")
• The Blind Spot: If the background room shrinks continuously at the exact same rate the character grows, the player's brain loses all sense of absolute size—it just looks like nothing is changing.
• The Fix: Keep small, recognizable micro-details (floor tiles, chair rivets, a coffee mug, doorway trim, or 2.5D dust motes) at fixed proportions during each growth tier, and swap/scale the room in distinct architectural milestones (e.g., Room -> Warehouse -> Courtyard) rather than infinitely shrinking one room.

Player Control vs. Game Director Responsibilities

Keeping a strict boundary between what the player controls and what your C# Director automates makes the game intuitive and stress-free:
• What the Player Controls (Simple & Rewarding):
	• Exploration & Item/Amplifier Use: Gathering points passively or actively across the level.
	• Initiating Rest / Triggering Events: Choosing when and where to rest (e.g., in an open lab corner vs. near a doorframe or resting chair) to cash in queued growth points.
	• Stealth / Navigation Choices: Moving through the level, ducking/hiding behind cover, or choosing escape routes once they become too big to blend in.
 What Your Camera & NPC Director Automates (Zero Player Micromanagement):
	• Automated Framing During Rest / Queue Execution: When GrowthEventQueue fires, the camera smoothly glides to the optimal measuring angle (e.g., framing a nearby wall marker, doorway, or Adult NPC in the background) so the player clearly sees whether they grew "just a tiny bit" or "tripled in size."
	• Automatic Eye-Line & Reaction Cuts: As the character's scale crosses key thresholds (like matching or passing the Adult NPC's height), the camera automatically prioritizes two-shot framing (capturing both the Adult's shifting gaze/intimidated posture and the Child's growing silhouette) without the player touching the camera stick/mouse.
	• Adaptive Gameplay Camera Compensation: Once the rest event finishes and the player resumes exploring, the camera automatically recalculates its follow distance (+Z), elevation (+Y), FOV, and Near/Far clipping planes to match the character's new size—so movement always feels natural, heavy, and unobstructed.

These are mathematical examples, not proposed game progression values.

We could assign each world context its own reference value \(W\).

The relative relationship becomes:
\[
R = 2^{P-W}
\]

Where:
- \(P\) = persistent player growth.
- \(W\) = world reference scale.
- \(R\) = player's relative size within that world.

This provides a way to describe the player as small, normal-sized or enormous relative to the environment without requiring the active simulation to use those enormous physical dimensions.

If \(P-W=0\), the player is at the world's reference size.
If \(P-W=5\), the player is 32 times that reference size.
If \(P-W=-5\), the player is 1/32 of that reference size.

We can also use this relationship to select environmental detail levels and presentation behavior.

7. An important opportunity: environmental detail can follow relative scale
Your idea that the returning town could have minimized details is especially interesting.

We could make environment generation respond to the player's relative scale.

For example:
- A book could initially have individual readable pages and interactive objects.
- As the player becomes enormous relative to it, the book could become a simpler visual object.
- A bookshelf could transition from individually simulated books to grouped geometry.
- Buildings could transition between detailed structures and larger simplified representations.
- At astronomical scales, planets could be represented by specialized procedural or impostor rendering techniques.
This is more than ordinary level-of-detail optimization.

It is scale-aware world representation.

The world doesn't simply become smaller. Its representation changes according to the relationship between the player and the environment.
\[
S_{\text{local}} =
\operatorname{clamp}
\left(
e^{P-W}\cdot B\cdot E,\,
S_{\min},S_{\max}
\right)
\]

Where:
- \(P\) is absolute growth.
- \(W\) is the world reference.
- \(B\) is the temporary modifier.
- \(E\) is the event modifier.
A logistic function is one candidate for a bounded growth phase:
\[
G(t)=\frac{L}{1+e^{-k(t-t_0)}}
\]
However, because your game ultimately allows progression into astronomical scales, I would not make the entire progression globally bounded by one logistic curve. We could use separate growth phases or piecewise functions, each with its own parameters.

This gives us opportunities for gameplay challenges without imposing an artificial permanent ceiling.
